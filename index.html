Sources and Methodology

Full transparency on the data, frameworks and academic research behind this assessment tool.

I. Theoretical Foundation

This tool synthesizes five readiness dimensions from established peer-reviewed instruments in entrepreneurship research:

  1. Strategic Readiness from the Entrepreneurial Orientation (EO) construct. Peer-reviewed
    Lumpkin, G.T. & Dess, G.G. (1996). "Clarifying the Entrepreneurial Orientation Construct and Linking It to Performance." Academy of Management Review, 21(1), 135-172.
  2. Operational Readiness from the RISE model's technical and managerial skill domains. Peer-reviewed
    Lyons, T.S., Lyons, J.S. & Jolley, G.J. (2019). "The Readiness Inventory for Successful Entrepreneurship (RISE): A Tool for University Engagement in Entrepreneurial Learning." Journal of Economic Development in Higher Education, 2.
  3. Financial Awareness from the Financial Literacy framework. Peer-reviewed
    Lusardi, A. & Mitchell, O.S. (2014). "The Economic Importance of Financial Literacy." Journal of Economic Literature, 52(1), 5-44.
  4. Entrepreneurial Mindset from the Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy (ESE) construct. Peer-reviewed
    Chen, C.C., Greene, P.G. & Crick, A. (1998). "Does Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy Distinguish Entrepreneurs from Managers?" Journal of Business Venturing, 13(4), 295-316.
  5. Implementation Capacity from Effectuation Theory and ESE action dimensions. Peer-reviewed
    Sarasvathy, S.D. (2001). "Causation and Effectuation." Academy of Management Review, 26(2), 243-263.

II. Supporting Meta-Analyses and Reviews

Pattern detection rules and scoring interpretation are supported by the following meta-analytic evidence:

  1. Rauch, A. & Frese, M. (2007). "Let's Put the Person Back into Entrepreneurship Research: A Meta-Analysis on the Relationship Between Business Owners' Personality Traits, Business Creation, and Success." European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology, 16(4), 353-385. Meta-analysis of 116 studies. Found that traits matched to entrepreneurial tasks (need for achievement, self-efficacy, innovativeness, stress tolerance, proactive personality) predict both business creation and success. Peer-reviewed
  2. Zhao, H., Seibert, S.E. & Hills, G.E. (2005). "The Mediating Role of Self-Efficacy in the Development of Entrepreneurial Intentions." Journal of Applied Psychology, 90(6), 1265-1272. Found that entrepreneurial self-efficacy fully mediates the effects of entrepreneurship courses, prior experience and risk propensity on entrepreneurial intentions. Peer-reviewed
  3. Ericsson, K.A., Krampe, R.T. & Tesch-Romer, C. (1993). "The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance." Psychological Review, 100(3), 363-406. Foundational paper establishing that targeted practice on specific weaknesses produces faster skill development than general practice. Peer-reviewed
  4. Aldrich, H.E. & Martinez, M.A. (2001). "Many Are Called, but Few Are Chosen: An Evolutionary Perspective for the Study of Entrepreneurship." Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice, 25(4), 41-56. Argued that organizing processes (building teams, acquiring resources, creating structures) are as important as opportunity recognition for entrepreneurial success. Peer-reviewed
  5. Weinberg, F.J. & Hausfeld, M. (2024). "The role of relational mechanisms in the executive coaching process on client outcomes in distance coaching relationships." Journal of Managerial Psychology, 39(6), 695-715. Found that coaching readiness predicts learning and performance improvement outcomes in coaching engagements. Peer-reviewed

III. Mixed-Method Assessment Design

This tool uses three question formats per dimension: Likert scales (1-5), multiple choice scenario questions and open-ended reflection prompts. This mixed-method approach surfaces gaps that a single question format would miss. Open-ended questions reveal confidence vs. competence gaps and provide qualitative consulting hooks. Two original question types unique to this tool measure consulting readiness and coachability.

Scale design methodology informed by: DeVellis, R.F. (2016). "Scale Development: Theory and Applications." 4th ed., SAGE Publications. Peer-reviewed

IV. Maturity Model

Instead of comparing your scores to external benchmark averages that become outdated, this tool uses a competency maturity model grounded in peer-reviewed entrepreneurial development research:

  1. Entrepreneurial Development System: Lichtenstein, G.A. & Lyons, T.S. (2001). "The Entrepreneurial Development System: Transforming Business Talent and Community Economies." Economic Development Quarterly, 15(1), 3-20. Proposed a skill-based framework for assessing and developing entrepreneurial competencies across progressive levels. Peer-reviewed
  2. Skill-Based Development: Kutzhanova, N., Lyons, T.S. & Lichtenstein, G.A. (2009). "Skill-Based Development of Entrepreneurs and the Role of Personal and Peer Group Coaching in Enterprise Development." Economic Development Quarterly, 23(3), 193-210. Extended the competency progression model with empirical evidence for coaching-based skill development. Peer-reviewed
  3. RISE Framework: Lyons, T.S., Lyons, J.S. & Jolley, G.J. (2019). "The Readiness Inventory for Successful Entrepreneurship (RISE): A Tool for University Engagement in Entrepreneurial Learning." Journal of Economic Development in Higher Education, 2. Applied the maturity model specifically to entrepreneurial readiness assessment. Peer-reviewed

Why a maturity model instead of benchmarks: External benchmarks (e.g. average scores from a specific study) are snapshots of a specific population at a specific time. They become outdated and do not apply equally to all entrepreneurs. A maturity model defines progressive competency levels that remain valid regardless of when or where the assessment is taken. Your score maps to a level (Unaware, Aware, Developing, Competent, Advanced) with descriptors specific to each dimension.

V. Maturity Levels

  1. Advanced (4.1-5.0): Strong demonstrated capability. Ready to scale, mentor others or optimize. Corresponds to the highest skill tier in Lichtenstein & Lyons (2001).
  2. Competent (3.3-4.0): Solid foundation with evidence of capability. Can absorb and implement consulting recommendations effectively.
  3. Developing (2.5-3.2): Early progress with identifiable gaps. Focused skill-building will yield significant returns.
  4. Aware (1.9-2.4): Recognizes the importance of this dimension but has not taken meaningful action. Foundational work needed.
  5. Unaware (1.0-1.8): Has not engaged with this dimension. Critical foundational gaps exist.

Source: Adapted from Lichtenstein & Lyons (2001) and Kutzhanova et al. (2009) competency progression frameworks.

VI. Coachability and Consulting Readiness (Original)

This tool includes questions specifically designed to measure readiness for a consulting engagement. These are not found in standard ESE or EO instruments. They assess:

  1. Prior experience implementing professional advice
  2. Willingness to act on recommendations even when they conflict with existing beliefs
  3. Preparedness to brief a consultant with organized information

These questions are original to this tool and have not been validated through independent psychometric testing. Their purpose is diagnostic: to help the consultant (and the entrepreneur) gauge whether a consulting engagement is likely to produce results given the entrepreneur's current disposition toward external guidance. Research by Weinberg and Hausfeld (2024) found that coaching readiness directly predicts learning outcomes and work performance improvements.

Theoretical grounding: Weinberg, F.J. & Hausfeld, M. (2024). "The role of relational mechanisms in the executive coaching process on client outcomes in distance coaching relationships." Journal of Managerial Psychology, 39(6), 695-715. Found that coaching readiness directly predicts personal learning and work performance improvements in coaching engagements. Peer-reviewed

VII. Action Plan Methodology

The structured action plan (This Week / This Month / This Quarter) is prioritized by the user's weakest dimensions. The progressive structure is grounded in:

  1. Lichtenstein & Lyons (2001): Progressive skill-building methodology where competencies are developed in stages.
  2. Ericsson et al. (1993): Deliberate practice framework showing that targeted improvement on specific weak points produces faster development than general skill-building.

VIII. Environmental Context

When a region is selected, the results include structural environmental conditions that interact with the user's dimension scores. These are qualitative conditions (not statistical benchmarks) drawn from:

  1. GEM (2026). Global Entrepreneurship Monitor 2025/2026 Global Report: From Uncertainty to Opportunity. Structural conditions around entrepreneurial activity, fear of failure and ecosystem support across 53 economies. Peer-reviewed
  2. WIPO (2024). Global Innovation Index 2024: Unlocking the Promise of Social Entrepreneurship. 17th Edition. Innovation ecosystem rankings and structural conditions across 133 economies. Peer-reviewed
  3. World Bank (2024). Business Ready (B-READY) 2024. Regulatory framework and public services assessment replacing the discontinued Ease of Doing Business index. Institutional

These conditions describe structural realities (e.g. "limited early-stage capital availability," "strong regulatory transparency") that remain directionally stable over time. They are not numerical benchmarks and do not require annual updates to remain valid.

IX. Limitations

  1. This is a screening tool, not a diagnostic instrument. It has not been independently validated through psychometric testing (e.g. test-retest reliability, convergent validity).
  2. Self-report bias: responses are subject to self-serving bias and social desirability effects.
  3. Maturity levels are based on competency progression frameworks from entrepreneurship research (Lichtenstein & Lyons, 2001; Kutzhanova et al., 2009). The specific score cutpoints (4.1, 3.3, 2.5, 1.9) are adapted from these frameworks and applied to a 5-point scale.
  4. All scored questions use graduated scales (Likert 1-5 or weighted multiple choice). This avoids the distortion caused by binary scoring and produces smoother dimension averages.
  5. Open-ended questions are not scored numerically. They provide qualitative context for consulting conversations.
  6. Consulting readiness and coachability questions are original to this tool and have not been externally validated.
  7. Source currency: The maturity model approach avoids the outdating problem inherent in external benchmarks. The competency progression framework (Lichtenstein & Lyons, 2001) defines developmental stages that remain valid regardless of when the assessment is taken.
